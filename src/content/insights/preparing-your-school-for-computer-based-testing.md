Computer-based testing (CBT) can take a great deal of work off teachers and examination officers. Objective questions are marked instantly, scores do not have to be copied by hand, and results are ready as soon as an exam closes.

The schools that get those benefits are the ones that prepare properly. Most CBT problems on exam day are not software problems. They are a computer that will not start, a network that drops, a student who cannot log in or a question bank typed in a hurry the night before.

This checklist covers the groundwork.

## 1. Count your computers honestly

Start with the number of computers that actually work, not the number in the laboratory. For each machine, check that it powers on, has a working keyboard and mouse, and can open a web browser.

Then work out how many students you need to test and how many sessions that means. If you have 30 working computers and 120 students sitting a paper, you need at least four sessions, plus time between them. Plan for one or two machines failing on the day.

## 2. Decide whether you need the internet at all

A CBT system does not have to run over the internet. Many schools are better served by a system that runs on a server computer inside the school, with student computers connecting over the local network. Exams then carry on even when the internet connection or the data bundle does not.

If you do use an online system, test it at the busiest time of day with as many students as you expect, not with one teacher in an empty lab.

## 3. Check the network and the power

On a local system, every student computer must be able to reach the server reliably. Test this by logging in on every machine at the same time, the way students will.

Power matters as much as the network. Know what happens if the electricity goes off mid-exam: is there a backup supply for the server and the network equipment, and does the system save answers as students go?

## 4. Prepare the question bank early

Typing and checking questions takes longer than most schools expect. Give subject teachers a clear format and a deadline well before exams. Have a second person proofread each paper on screen, where formatting mistakes are easier to spot than on paper.

Decide in advance whether questions and options will be shuffled, how long each paper lasts and whether students can return to earlier questions.

## 5. Set up students and subjects carefully

Every student needs a login and must see only the exams for their own class and subjects. Errors here cause the most confusion on the day, so check the student list and subject combinations against your school records before the first exam.

Choose a login method students can manage. Admission numbers with a short PIN work well, provided the system limits repeated failed attempts.

## 6. Train staff and give students a practice run

Invigilators need to know how to start an exam session, help a student who cannot log in and what to do if a computer fails. A short walkthrough for staff prevents most exam-day delays.

Give students at least one practice test. Many will be using the system, and some will be using a computer, for the first time. A practice session turns exam-day nerves about the technology into a non-issue.

## 7. Plan exam day

Write a simple running order: who opens the lab, who starts the server, who checks each computer before students arrive, and who is responsible for results at the end. Keep a printed list of student logins in case someone forgets theirs, and store it securely.

## 8. Protect results and student data

Results and student records are personal information. Limit who can access the admin area, use strong passwords for staff accounts, and back up results after each exam session to a separate location.

## Getting started

If your school is considering CBT, begin with an honest look at your equipment and network. That assessment usually shows whether the next step is a new system, some repairs and reorganisation, or both.
